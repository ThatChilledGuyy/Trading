Quick tools

Five rules to remember

  1. Never enter without a stop-loss and a position size set from your risk budget.
  2. Trade one written setup until you have enough results to judge it.
  3. Check the exact pair, spread and depth before every trade.
  4. Record every trade, including setups you skipped.
  5. Stay on demo until your journal shows positive expectancy after costs.

Part 2: CoinMarketCap guide

Use it as a funnel: broad market, then shortlist, then the actual pair and chart. It is a research starting point, not a signal generator.

2.1 Understand the overall market
Review total market cap, total volume and its change, BTC and ETH performance, Bitcoin dominance, sector performance, and whether gains are broad or concentrated. Label the environment bullish, bearish, ranging, mixed or event-driven, and record the evidence. BTC and ETH rising while most altcoins fall points to a market concentrated in large assets, not a broad altcoin rally.
2.2 Screen with rankings
Keep separate groups: large liquid assets for consistent practice, mid-caps with adequate activity, sector assets you understand, and a higher-risk list for small or newly listed coins. Start with a manageable liquid list.

A high volume-to-cap ratio does not prove good liquidity. Check venue, spread, depth and the quality of reported volume.
2.3 Read price change and volume together
A 20% gain may be a breakout, a news reaction or the end of a speculative move. A 2% gain may be a base forming or an inactive market. Compare equal time windows, volume against its own baseline, performance against BTC, distance to support and resistance, and the real chart structure. A potential runner needs several independent conditions: relative strength, rising activity, clear structure, room to the next level, acceptable liquidity and no rule-breaking event risk.
2.4 Market cap, supply and FDV
  • Circulating supply: tokens reported as circulating.
  • Market cap: price × circulating supply.
  • FDV: price × fully diluted supply estimate.
Example: $2 price, 100M circulating, 1B maximum. Market cap = $200M, FDV = $2B, ten times larger. That does not prove overvaluation; it tells you to investigate unlocks, emissions, recipients and whether demand can absorb new supply. For short-term trades, unlocks matter if they fall inside your holding period.
2.5 Volume is not liquidity
Open the token's listed markets, pick the exact exchange and pair, check the live spread and depth near entry and exit, estimate slippage at your size, and verify minimum order rules. Never assume combined market volume is available on your venue.
2.6 Sectors and categories
Use categories (AI, DeFi, infrastructure, gaming) to find themes. If several AI tokens outperform BTC with rising relative volume and one has a clean 4H breakout structure, that one deserves investigation. Sector momentum does not make every token a buy; check each individually.

2.8 Detect extended coins
Ask: has price broken a defined base and held the breakout level? How far is it from its 20 EMA or VWAP? How much room to the next resistance? Is the move large relative to ATR? Is there a pullback entry with a sensible stop? Chasing a strong asset can leave too little reward for the risk.
2.9 Verify asset and pair
CoinMarketCap is not your execution venue; prices differ by venue, pair and timing. Verify the token and contract address, confirm the exact pair, use that venue's data for entries, stops and sizing, and journal the exchange, pair and timestamp. Critical for new tokens, wrapped assets and similar names.
2.10 Build a scanner with the API
The CoinMarketCap API offers market data and metadata subject to plan and rate limits: ID, symbol, price, percent changes, market cap, supply, volume, category and timestamp. Combine it with exchange candles to compute your own relative strength, volume expansion, ATR and technical conditions. The output is a ranked research list with evidence, not a pump prediction.

3.3 Relative strength
R = (P_end − P_start) / P_start × 100% over exactly the same interval. BTC $100,000 → $103,000 is +3%; Coin A $10 → $10.80 is +8%; excess return is about 5 percentage points. A rising Coin/BTC ratio means outperformance. It identifies candidates, not entries; an asset can outperform BTC and still be in a downtrend.
3.4 Open interest, funding and liquidations
  • Open interest: outstanding derivatives exposure. Rising OI does not reveal direction; every contract has a long and a short side.
  • Funding: periodic payment that helps align perpetuals with the reference market. Check each exchange's rules.
  • Liquidations: reported forced closures. Heatmaps are estimates, not verified maps.

These are descriptions, not signals. Combine with structure, spot activity and funding.
3.5 Three scanner outputs
Ready for chart review (meets screening), Watch for trigger (interesting, no entry yet), Reject (liquidity, structure, target space or event risk). Explain why each qualifies and what would disqualify it.

4.2 Mark structure before indicators
Identify swing highs and lows, label structure, mark support and resistance and the nearest opposing level. Repeat on 4H, then use 1H for entry. Vocabulary: HH, HL, LH, LL, BOS, CHoCH/MSS. These terms vary between methods, so write down your exact rule for confirming a swing and a break.
4.3 Volume Profile
POC is the highest-volume price, VAH and VAL bound the value area, high-volume nodes show concentrated activity and low-volume nodes show thin activity. Example: BTC ranges $98,000 to $100,000 with POC $99,100, VAL $98,700, VAH $99,600, then breaks above $100,000. Do not buy immediately. Watch for a hold above the range, follow-through and a valid retest. Falling back into the old value area suggests failure. POC is not a guaranteed magnet.
4.4 VWAP and 4.5 Alerts
Price above a rising session VWAP that pulls back, rejects and closes higher with bullish 1H structure may support a long if entry, stop and target rules are met. Repeated crossings of a flat VWAP suggest a rotational market where trend strategies struggle. Set alerts for swing highs and lows, pullback zones, closes beyond structure, invalidation and targets. An alert is a reminder to analyse, not an entry order.

5.1 and 5.2 Setup, entry, stop, target
Pick one defined setup: breakout and retest, trend pullback, sweep then structure shift, range rejection or momentum continuation. If you cannot state the trigger exactly, it is not ready to test.

Example: entry $10.10, stop $9.80, target $10.80. Risk $0.30, reward $0.70, gross RR ≈ 2.33 before fees, funding and slippage. Put the stop where the thesis is invalid, never to manufacture a ratio.

5.3 Position size from risk
Quantity = risk budget / entry-to-stop distance. With $1,000 equity and 0.5% risk, the budget is $5; $5 / $0.30 ≈ 16.67 tokens, about $168 notional. Size comes before leverage. Reduce for fees, slippage and funding. Inverse contracts, FX, futures and synthetics need contract-specific formulas. Planned risk is not maximum loss: gaps, slippage and liquidation can cost more. Try the calculator.
5.4 Manage after entry
Decide beforehand: fixed or rule-adjusted stop, partial profits, fixed target or trail, exit on opposite signal, and effects of funding, swaps or expiry. Never widen a stop because a trade is losing or move a target out of attachment.

Part 6: Liquidity, order blocks, FVGs, candles

These are vulnerable to hindsight. Keep definitions fixed during testing and compare against a baseline.

6.1 Liquidity pools and sweeps
Mark previous day and week highs and lows, equal highs and lows, major swings, range boundaries and session extremes. Bullish sweep hypothesis: price trades below a swing low, closes back above, breaks a local swing high, holds a retest, and has space to the next level. Reverse for bearish. Sweeps can fail; confirmation and risk control are essential.
6.2 Order blocks
Testable bullish definition: the last bearish candle before a bullish displacement that closes above a predefined swing high. Specify the qualifying candle, swing confirmation, body or full range, invalidation and allowed retests. Do not assume it reveals institutional orders; test whether it improves a baseline.
6.3 Fair value gaps
Bullish FVG: Candle 3 low > Candle 1 high; the gap is that interval. Bearish: Candle 3 high < Candle 1 low. If Candle 1 high is $100 and Candle 3 low is $101, the gap is $100 to $101. Not every FVG fills and not every fill reverses.
6.4 Candlestick patterns
Patterns matter in context. A bullish engulfing candle at predefined support may qualify for review; mid-range chop has little value. Record pattern, location, timeframe, entry and outcome, and compare with a no-pattern baseline.

Leverage sets required margin, not how much you may lose. If correct sizing gives $250 notional for a $5 risk, do not raise it to $1,000 because the exchange allows it.
8. Forex
Use TradingView, an economic calendar, central-bank releases and your broker's spreads, swaps and pip values. For EUR/USD assess both currencies: ECB and Fed expectations, diverging rate paths, Daily trend, and imminent announcements. Example: Daily uptrend pulls back into a 4H support zone; on 1H price sweeps a local low, reclaims it, breaks a confirmed swing high and holds the retest, targeting the next 4H high. Before entry compute pip distance, pip value, size and costs, and check the spread and news. Do not apply crypto funding assumptions; swaps and rollover are the relevant costs.
9. Synthetic and volatility indices
Test each instrument on its own. Check the provider, historical behaviour, contract and point value, minimum size, spread, order rules, availability and volatility on your timeframe. Volatility 75 and Volatility 75 (1s) are not interchangeable. Example bearish pullback: 4H downtrend, rally into resistance, lower high, 1H break of a local low, invalidation above the swing, size from the exact point value. It is a hypothesis to test. Use demo accounts while learning; fast candles are not a reason to raise risk.
10. Stock indices and futures
Verify contract size, tick size and value, hours, initial and maintenance margin, expiry and rollover, and settlement. Example breakout: Daily or 4H context permits the direction, price breaks the prior range high, a predefined retest or continuation triggers entry, stop at invalidation, target adequate after costs, and no major scheduled event. Standard and micro contracts differ in tick value. Continuous charts may not match the tradable contract price.

Parts 11 and 12: Prove your edge and run your day

11.1 Write the rules first
Document market, timeframes, permitted regimes, exact entry, stop, target and exit, sizing method, maximum trade and daily risk, event filters, costs, and what invalidates the setup. Replace "enter when momentum is strong" with measurable conditions.
11.3 Metrics
Expectancy = (Pw × W) − (Pl × L) in consistent units such as R, net of costs. Profit factor = gross profits / |gross losses|. Max drawdown is the largest peak-to-trough fall. Also track win rate, trade count, longest losing streak, cost burden, and results by regime, instrument and direction. A 70% win rate can lose money; a 40% win rate can profit. The journal computes these for you.
11.4 Test in sequence
  1. Historical study with fixed definitions
  2. Backtest with realistic costs
  3. Out-of-sample test
  4. Demo forward test
  5. Small live trial, only with affordable capital
Avoid changing several settings after each loss; that is overfitting.
12. Daily operating routine
Before: check the broad market and benchmark, screen candidates and liquidity, check news, releases and supply events, review derivatives, mark higher-timeframe levels, shortlist.
During: wait for price to reach the area, recheck context, confirm entry, calculate risk and costs, reject if rules fail, execute and manage by plan.
After: record every trade and qualifying miss, save screenshots, separate strategy losses from rule violations, update stats, review costs, choose one improvement to test.
Weekly: Which setups paid after costs? Which regimes lost? Was the scanner early enough? Did costs bite? Did indicators help in testing? Did I follow the rules? What is the next single hypothesis?

What trading is

Start here if you have never placed a trade.

The basic idea
Trading means buying or selling something whose price moves, hoping to profit from the move. You go long (buy) to profit when price rises, or short (sell) to profit when it falls. Every trade has an entry, a stop-loss that limits your loss, and a target or exit.
The five markets you will meet
MarketWhat you tradeGood to know
Crypto spotCoins you actually ownOpen 24/7. Simplest to start.
Crypto perpetualsContracts that track a coinLeverage and funding fees add risk.
ForexCurrency pairs like EUR/USDMoves with economic news and interest rates.
Synthetic indicesBroker-made simulated marketsTest each instrument on demo first.
Stock indicesUS500, Nasdaq and similarSession hours and scheduled news matter.
Pick one market and stay with it. Crypto spot or a major forex pair is the easiest to learn on.
Words you need first
  • Spread: the small cost between buy and sell price.
  • Leverage: borrowed exposure. It raises both gains and losses.
  • Stop-loss: an exit order that caps your loss.
  • Lot or size: how much you trade.
Every term is in the glossary.
Be realistic. Most retail traders lose money, and skill takes months to build. Only ever risk money you can afford to lose. No course, signal or tool guarantees profit.

Set up your account safely

A small, safe setup you can finish in an afternoon.

  1. Choose one market for your first three months.
  2. Create a free TradingView account for charts.
  3. Open a demo account with a regulated broker, or use an exchange's practice mode. Check the regulator on the regulator's own website, not the broker's.
  4. Secure everything: unique passwords, two-factor authentication, and never share keys. If you use an API key, make it read-only or without withdrawal rights.
  5. Open the journal in this site and log your first demo trade.
  6. Block out a fixed time each day to study or trade.
Red flags: guaranteed returns, paid signal groups, strangers who offer to manage your money, "send crypto to unlock profit", and brokers with no verifiable regulator.

Reading a chart

What every candle, line and label on the screen means.

  • Candle: shows open, high, low and close for one time period. The thin lines are wicks.
  • Timeframe: how long each candle lasts. A 4H chart has one candle per four hours.
  • Trend: higher highs and higher lows is an uptrend; lower highs and lower lows is a downtrend.
  • Support and resistance: price areas where it has repeatedly paused or turned.
  • Volume: how much traded in the period.

Risk comes before profit

The rules that keep you in the game long enough to improve.

  • Risk a small, fixed share of your account per trade. Beginners: 0.25% to 1%.
  • Place the stop-loss first, then size the position from it.
  • Set a daily loss limit and stop for the day when you hit it.
  • Never add to a losing trade. Never move a stop further away.

Why small losses matter

Deep losses are hard to recover from: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain just to get back to even.

Psychology and rules

Most blown accounts come from broken rules, not bad analysis.

MistakeRule that prevents it
Revenge trading after a lossTwo losses in a row means stop for the day.
OvertradingSet a maximum number of trades per day.
Fear of missing outNo chasing. Wait for the written trigger or skip.
Moving the stopThe stop is placed once and only tightened by a written rule.
Oversizing after a winPosition size comes from the formula every time.
  • Write an if-then plan before the session: "If price does X, I do Y. If not, I do nothing."
  • Trading nothing is a valid result. Log the setups you skipped.
  • Review your journal weekly and change one thing at a time.

Get funded

How funded-trader programs work, how to judge whether you are ready, and how to plan a challenge.

What "funded" means
A proprietary trading (prop) firm lets you trade its capital, often on a simulated account, after you pass an evaluation. You keep a share of the profits, commonly 70% to 90%. You pay an evaluation fee, usually non-refundable, and you must follow strict rules. Funded does not mean guaranteed income, and many people fail challenges.
How a typical challenge works
RuleTypical range
Phase 1 profit target8% to 10%
Phase 2 profit targetAbout 5%
Max daily loss3% to 5%
Max total loss6% to 12%
Minimum trading daysOften 3 to 5
Restricted behaviourNews trading, martingale, copy trading, weekend holds, bots
These ranges are illustrative. Rules differ by firm, so read each firm's current rules in full. Check whether the drawdown is static (from your starting balance) or trailing (from your highest equity), and whether it uses balance or equity.
How to choose a firm
  • Clear, written rules with no hidden conditions.
  • Evidence of payouts and realistic payout timelines.
  • Offers the instruments you actually trade.
  • Fair fees and a reset or retry policy you understand.
  • Independent reviews, not only the firm's own marketing or paid affiliates.
A sensible passing plan
  1. Pass the readiness gates below on demo first.
  2. Run the planner to see your odds before you pay.
  3. Risk 0.25% to 0.5% per trade, not the maximum the firm allows.
  4. Use one setup and one or two trades per day.
  5. Set your own daily stop at half the firm's daily limit.
  6. Avoid major news. When the target and minimum days are done, stop and submit.
After you are funded
Keep the same risk that got you there. Withdraw on schedule, keep records for tax purposes, and scale only when the numbers support it. Treat each failed challenge as a cost of doing business and budget for it.
